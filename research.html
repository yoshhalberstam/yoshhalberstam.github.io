1) Social networks and political information

Who sees political information, and how fast

Data: links and messages among 2.2 million politically engaged Twitter users, about 90 million connections
Homophily, Group Size, and the Diffusion of Political Information in Social Networks: Evidence from Twitter Journal of Public Economics (2016) · with Brian Knight
Paper Online appendix
  • A network model predicts more exposure to like-minded information, and more connections and information for larger groups.
  • On Twitter, majority-group users get more information, sooner; like-minded information reaches like-minded users faster.
Methods: network model; empirical analysis of links, messages and exposure timing
2) Behavioral measurement from voice recordings

Pitch patterns that averages hide

Data: about 40,000 three-second voicemail greetings from lawyers at top U.S. law firms
Gender Identity and Mode-Switching Behavior: Evidence from the Human Voice Working paper (updated November 2026)
Paper Online appendix
  • Within a single greeting, about a third of women switch between their usual pitch and a second, near where men's voices sit.
  • Women associates switch more than partners and counsel (43 vs 31 percent); listeners rate women who switch as less senior.
Methods: pitch tracking, Gaussian mixture models, survey experiment; Python
3) Labor market institutions and pay

Pay transparency and unions in Canadian universities

Data: administrative salary records for Canadian university faculty
Pay Transparency and the Gender Gap American Economic Journal: Applied Economics (2023) · with Michael Baker, Kory Kroft, Alexandre Mas, Derek Messacar
Paper Online appendix
  • Provincial laws made faculty salaries above a threshold public, adopted at different times across provinces.
  • The laws narrowed the gender pay gap by about 20 to 40 percent.
Methods: event-study design using variation across institutions and departments
The Impact of Unions on the Wage Distribution: Evidence from Higher Education American Economic Review: Insights (2026) · with Michael Baker, Kory Kroft, Alexandre Mas, Derek Messacar
Paper Online appendix
  • Unionization raised salaries about 10 percent at the bottom of the distribution and left the top unaffected.
  • Evidence points to contractual salary floors; employment changed little, and higher enrollment paid for the wage increase.
Methods: staggered-rollout design, distributional analysis
